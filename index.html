Why this matters

NASA's Artemis astronauts and a wave of robotic landers are heading to the lunar south pole, where water ice hides in craters that never see the Sun. Down there the Sun never climbs more than about 2° above level. It circles the horizon once a month, so a single hill can throw a site into days of darkness, and mountains can hide Earth and cut off communication. A site that is perfect on one date can leave a lander dark and silent one week later.

Lunar Lookout puts NASA's own laser-altimeter terrain (LRO LOLA) and the real motion of the Sun and Earth into one tool. Mission planners, students and space fans can all see, hour by hour, whether a landing site gets sunlight for power and keeps Earth in view for radio contact.

Lander power

Pick a site and a landing time. Everything below recalculates from real orbital mechanics: where the Sun and Earth are in the lunar sky, and whether hills block them. "Lander power" describes your spacecraft: the solar array charges the battery when the Sun is up, and the battery has to carry the power draw through every night. If it runs flat, the lander dies; that drives the go / no-go call.

At the south pole the Sun never climbs more than about 1.5° because the Moon's axis is tilted only 1.5°. It circles the whole horizon once a month, so a hill in any direction can cast a long shadow. Earth wobbles up and down by about 7° (libration) in roughly the direction of the lunar near side.

Mission readiness

The Landing Site Score adds up three parts: power survival (40 points, scaled by the lowest battery level and zero if it ever runs flat), communication (35 points × the share of time Earth is in view) and sunlight (25 points × the share of time the Sun is up). Sunlight % counts how much of the Sun's disk clears the local horizon (it is about 0.5° wide, so partial sunrise matters). Earth in view means the center of Earth is above the terrain, so a direct antenna link is possible without a relay satellite.

Sites a few kilometers apart can differ by days of darkness. Ridges and crater rims see the Sun more often; crater floors near the pole can stay dark for billions of years, which is why water ice survives there.

What if the launch slips?

Hardware delays move landing dates. Because the Sun and Earth both follow monthly cycles, a one-week slip can turn a sunny landing into one that starts in a long night.

Real terrain: LOLA elevation data

Loading NASA LOLA terrain…

The app loads NASA's LOLA 240 m south-pole elevation model automatically. For sharper detail within 5° of the pole, download LDEM_85S_40M.IMG (115 MB) from the LOLA polar GDR archive and drop it here. It stays in your browser.

LOLA is the laser altimeter on NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. It measured the height of the lunar surface billions of times. From those heights we trace a line in every direction to find how high the terrain rises, which gives each site its true horizon.

One-year runs from 1 Jan 2026 with a 2 m mast. The Sun and Earth positions also agree with an independent lunar libration model to within 0.03°, and the terrain ray tracer matches a hand-calculated case to within 0.1°. Run npm test and npm run test:real in the source code to reproduce every number.

Method: terrain is NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter LOLA polar elevation model (LDEM_75S_240M). Sun and Moon positions come from astronomy-engine (VSOP87 / ELP-based, ~1 arcminute), rotated into the Moon's body-fixed frame with the IAU/WGCCRE lunar orientation model (aligned with LOLA's Mean-Earth frame to well under 0.1°). Terrain horizons are ray-traced every 0.5° of azimuth out to 150 km, including lunar curvature. Sunlight uses the fraction of the solar disk above the horizon. The power model flies the mission hour by hour: a vertical Sun-tracking solar array charges the battery, which carries a constant load through each night. Landing Site Score = 40 × lowest battery level (0 if it runs flat) + 35 × Earth-in-view share + 25 × sunlight share. 3D view uses three.js. Landing-site coordinates are approximate; verify in LROC Quickmap. Built for NASA Space Apps 2026, challenge: Lunar Landing Site Navigator. Source code.
